How I approach problems

Three stories that share how I think and approach problems.

Growth

Getting 80% of new signups through the job portal's onboarding

The problem. Our job portal needed three answers from every new user: their field, their experience level, and whether they wanted remote jobs only. We used these to match people to jobs. The question was how to ask without people leaving.

What I did. I assumed asking one question per screen would work best, because that feels lighter when you fill out a long form. So I tested it against putting all three questions on a single screen. We ran each version for a few days at a time, for about a month.

What happened. The single screen won. Most people who left the step-by-step version left on the very first question. They had just signed up, and one more screen felt like an extra ask. With the final flow, 70% of people who clicked Apply on a job signed up, and 80% of them finished onboarding.

What I learned: when someone comes to do one thing, every extra screen costs you. Group the asks together instead of spreading them out.

Founder mode

Launching a subscription with no payment system and no pricing

The problem. The subscription product was ready, but there was no payment gateway on the site and no price decided. The founder had to leave suddenly. The team was tired after a long sprint, and only one junior developer was around.

What I did. I decided not to wait. I set up a simple PayPal payment link and picked the prices myself: 999 rupees for India and 19.99 dollars for everyone else. Then we launched.

What happened. It wasn't pretty, but people paid. That proved the model worked and gave the founder something real to build on when he came back. The subscription went on to reach 40 free trials a day within three months.

What I learned: a rough launch that tests the real question beats a polished one that ships a month later.

AI

Why our AI videos were boring, and how I fixed it

The problem. Our video pipeline worked, but the videos were flat. The pictures moved and the words were right, but nothing felt like a story.

What I did. I traced it back to the direction agent, the one that plans each scene. Its instructions were full of technical rules meant to help the later agents write code. With all that in its head, it played safe and wrote dull, engineering-style scripts. I cut its context down to what a director actually needs, like story, pacing, and visuals, and gave it better examples to learn from.

What happened. The scripts started reading like actual video plans, with a hook, a build-up, and scenes that changed with the story. I tracked how many scenes still needed manual editing or creative fixes after a video was generated. That went from about 6 in every 10 scenes to 3.

What I learned: more context does not make AI better. Each agent should only know what its own job needs.
